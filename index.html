Color vision / A field guide

Did you know 7% of boys are colorblind—and they got it from their mama?

A mother, her son, and the eye appointment that made a familiar question look different. Explore the plates, the science, the inheritance, and what changes at school.

By RN Collins · September 2026

The hook is a question, not a universal statistic. The doctor’s 6–7% shorthand varies by population, and a maternal X pathway is possible without proving our family’s genotype. This is an actual historic plate, not an online eye exam. Screen settings, brightness, compression, and lighting can change what you see. An eye-care professional can test a child with standardized materials. This is not the plate Kai saw.

01 / The moment

Same page. Different picture.

Kai’s words, as RN recalls them

“I think my brain isn’t smart with colors.”

His brain was never the problem. I had been asking him to identify a cue he could not reliably separate.

“Come on, focus. What color is this?”

I had said versions of that before. During his exam I could see the number on a dot plate. Kai described gray. The eye doctor identified a mild red–green color vision difference. I apologized for making effort the question.

The doctor said the other findings looked normal, no glasses were needed for this finding, and we should tell his teacher. Those are Kai’s exam findings, not a general promise about every child.

METHOD 01 / Experienced at appointment; exact plate edition unknown

Color-plate book

The question

Can the viewer separate a numeral or path from carefully chosen colored dots?

How the clinician uses it

A clinician presents several standardized pseudoisochromatic plates under specified illumination. The person names a figure or traces a path; some plates demonstrate the task while others probe a difference.

What a result can show

A pattern across plates screens for red–green discrimination. A missed page alone does not establish type, severity, a gene, or the full appearance of the world.

Limits and pitfalls

Lighting, aged print, viewing time, instructions, and numeracy matter. This authentic historic Ishihara image on an uncalibrated display is a teaching object, never a home diagnosis.

Where it came from

Ishihara introduced plates in the early twentieth century; embedded colored-dot figures became a widely used screening method.

Why it matters here

RN saw a figure where Kai described gray. The exact clinical book and score are not in the transcript.

METHOD 02 / Experienced at appointment; exact chart and score unknown

Mirror letter chart

The question

How small a letter or other optotype can each eye resolve?

How the clinician uses it

A mirror lengthens the optical path in a small room. The clinician presents successively smaller symbols at a known effective distance, typically checking eyes separately.

What a result can show

Visual acuity measures spatial detail. Good acuity does not establish typical color discrimination, depth perception, or all-around eye health. The transcript reports no glasses were needed but gives no numeric score.

Limits and pitfalls

Distance, occlusion, illumination, chart type, age, and correction change the measure. Flipping a web chart illustrates geometry but cannot assess acuity.

Where it came from

Standardized chart reading grew after Snellen’s nineteenth-century optotypes; mirrors adapt the optical distance to compact clinics.

Why it matters here

Kai said this mirror arrangement was his favorite part.

Evidence: AAO pediatric acuity ↗

METHOD 03 / Experienced at appointment; exact stereo test unknown

Butterfly and special glasses

The question

Can the two eyes combine different images into depth?

How the clinician uses it

A stereotest uses polarized or other special glasses to deliver slightly different pictures to each eye. A butterfly or hidden shape may appear raised if the binocular images are fused.

What a result can show

A response supports stereopsis at the disparity tested. No threshold or score appears in the transcript. This is separate from color discrimination.

Limits and pitfalls

Large pictorial targets can contain clues visible to one eye. A screen animation does not present controlled separate-eye images and cannot measure stereoacuity.

Where it came from

Nineteenth-century stereoscopy led to later clinical targets; modern random-dot designs reduce some non-stereo clues.

Why it matters here

Kai described the figure popping off the page and wanted to poke it.

METHOD 04 / Experienced at appointment; exact instrument unknown

Bright-light internal-eye check

The question

What do the visible retina and optic nerve look like?

How the clinician uses it

The doctor dimmed the room, used bright light, and said the retina, nerve, and inside of the eyes looked normal. This is consistent with a fundus examination; the transcript does not tell us the instrument, dilation status, or imaging method.

What a result can show

A normal appearance is reassuring within the structures and field actually examined. It cannot by itself establish normal cone pigments or negate a color-vision difference.

Limits and pitfalls

Direct, indirect, slit-lamp, and imaging methods show different views. We cannot infer a full retinal workup or rule out every possible acquired cause from this conversation.

Where it came from

The ophthalmoscope made the living retina visible in the nineteenth century; contemporary methods vary in field and magnification.

Why it matters here

This explains how the eye structures could look healthy while the color plate remained difficult.

Evidence: AAO ophthalmoscopy ↗

METHOD 05 / Comparison method; not confirmed in Kai’s appointment

Hue-cap ordering

The question

Can a person order neighboring colors smoothly, and where do mistakes cluster?

How the clinician uses it

Physical colored caps have fixed endpoints; the observer places movable caps into a gradual sequence. Farnsworth D-15 and 100 Hue variants differ in number, difficulty, and scoring.

What a result can show

Error direction and magnitude can help characterize color discrimination. The seven browser tiles illustrate the task but have no validated clinical score.

Limits and pitfalls

Display gamut, calibration, lighting, memory, and puzzle strategies change the web experience. Do not infer vision from completing it.

Where it came from

Dean Farnsworth developed cap tests in the twentieth century as assessment expanded beyond plates.

Why it matters here

A classroom color gradient should also have labels or numbered positions.

METHOD 06 / Comparison method; not confirmed in Kai’s appointment

Anomaloscope match

The question

What red–green light mixture matches a yellow reference, and over what range?

How the clinician uses it

The observer adjusts a mixture field and reference brightness in a calibrated optical instrument. Examiners compare mixture settings and acceptable match range against norms.

What a result can show

A Rayleigh match can help classify protan and deutan forms with more specificity than a single plate. A trained professional interprets the results.

Limits and pitfalls

Our browser sliders use RGB display pixels, not calibrated spectral primaries or radiance. Any visual match here has zero diagnostic value.

Where it came from

Rayleigh’s nineteenth-century matching principle later became a clinical instrument in Nagel’s anomaloscope.

Why it matters here

Different people can arrive at different matches even when both call a patch “yellow.”

BEYOND THIS APPOINTMENT

Other tools answer different clinical questions.

No single test is a complete map of color vision. The methods below are a field guide to the landscape, not claims about what Kai took or a recommendation to order every test.

HRR plates

Hardy–Rand–Rittler symbol plates can probe red–green and blue–yellow discrimination. Their symbols can be more accessible to children who cannot name numerals. Lighting and reduced contrast can affect performance. HRR comparison study ↗ Contrast study ↗

D-15 versus 100 Hue

Both are physical arrangement tasks, but the shorter D-15 and longer 100 Hue ask different levels of discrimination. A cap-test score needs its own validated arrangement and illumination; the seven-tile puzzle above is only an analogy. NEI methods ↗

Computerized thresholds and signal lights

Cambridge Colour Test and CAD protocols measure discrimination under controlled display conditions. Lantern tests ask a different, task-oriented question about identifying small colored lights. Occupational rules specify approved tests and cutoffs; an online color puzzle cannot substitute. Pilot-test comparison study ↗

Genetic testing

DNA analysis can refine an inherited L/M opsin account, especially when standard perceptual results are ambiguous. A genotype does not replace the child’s functional performance or clinician interpretation. Kai and RN did not report having genetic testing. Genotype and clinical testing study ↗

03 / The science

“Red–green” does not mean seeing only gray.

The retina’s cones use light-sensitive pigments to help distinguish colors. Changes in the genes for the long- or middle-wavelength pigments can make some shades of red, yellow, and green hard to tell apart. The exact experience depends on the type and severity. Kai’s “gray” described what he saw on a particular plate; it is not a simulation of everything he sees. [2]

Color vision and visual sharpness are different measures. Common red–green deficiencies affect color discrimination without necessarily reducing visual acuity. [2]

From wavelengths to experience

Light is a spectrum

Objects reflect distributions of wavelengths, not named colors. S, M, and L cones have broad overlapping sensitivities. No one cone sees only blue, green, or red; the visual system compares their responses.

The retina compares signals

L versus M and S versus combined L/M pathways encode differences. An altered L- or M-cone pigment changes many color distinctions, beyond a single red and green pair. MedlinePlus Genetics ↗

Different forms

Anomalous trichromacy has three functioning classes with altered separation; dichromacy lacks one functional class. Protan and deutan pathways differ. Blue–yellow and near-total color loss involve other mechanisms. Kai’s subtype is unknown. NEI types ↗

Inherited and acquired

Color differences may be present from birth or follow eye disease, injury, drugs, or neurologic causes. A new or changing problem needs professional evaluation. This family story suggests an inherited path without proving a molecular diagnosis. NEI causes ↗

What “gray” does not mean: Kai described one particular plate, not every object he sees. Screen filters are approximations of population models, not a window into his individual perception.

04 / The inheritance

How can Mom see the number and her son not?

The genes most often involved in inherited red–green color vision differences are on the X chromosome. In the usual XX/XY pattern, a son receives his X from his mother and his Y from his father. A mother with one changed copy can have typical color vision and pass that X to a son. A father does not pass his X chromosome to a son. This diagram shows one possible carrier-mother scenario, not Kai’s confirmed genotype. [3] [2]

Why can Mom see it?

A typical carrier example has another functioning opsin gene set. X inactivation creates a mosaic, and some carriers have measurable color differences. The simple probability model does not predict one person’s exact vision.

What “from Mom” leaves out

A son’s X usually comes from his mother in this model. This applies to common inherited X-linked red–green forms. Other color differences may arise through other inheritance or be acquired. No one established RN’s or Kai’s genotype. MedlinePlus Genetics ↗

Put a denominator beside “7%”

MedlinePlus estimates about 1 in 12 males and 1 in 200 females among people of Northern European ancestry for red–green deficiency; rates are lower in almost all other populations studied. The appointment’s 6–7% shorthand is not a universal rate for all boys. Population context ↗

05 / In practice

What changes when an adult knows?

A child can appear to be guessing, rushing, or inattentive when a task relies on a color distinction they cannot reliably see. That is why telling the teacher matters. For a color-coded map, chart, or classroom activity, a label, shape, pattern, or written instruction can carry the same information. The NEI says children may need help with some classroom activities; the American Optometric Association notes how heavily learning materials use color coding. [4] [5]

If you notice a pattern: ask an eye-care professional about color vision testing. A social-media plate cannot diagnose a child. [1]

Color-only instruction

“Follow the red line.”

A color-coded map, graph, or worksheet can hide the answer inside a distinction the student cannot use.

Same task, more access

“Follow Route A, the solid line.”

Keep color and add text, shape, pattern, or position. Tell the teacher what works instead of treating color naming as effort.

What about camouflage?

The doctor mentioned possible advantages in some camouflage tasks. A small human study (12 people with color vision deficiency and 12 with typical trichromacy) reported an advantage with particular color-camouflaged screen stimuli. Another study using simulated dichromatic viewing found an overall trichromat advantage in hidden bird and egg searches, with task-specific differences. That evidence does not justify saying Kai generally sees camouflage better. Study one ↗ Study two ↗

Use the finding at school

A teacher note

Kai has a red–green color vision difference identified at his eye appointment. Please pair color with words, symbols, patterns, or position in maps, graphs, art materials, and instructions. Ask which cues work for him on the actual task.

Daily life and work

Maps, produce, games, lab indicators, and some occupations can involve color distinctions. Driving and safety-critical job rules vary by role and place; the appointment’s comments are not universal eligibility findings.

Questions for the clinician

Which plate book and score? Was each eye tested separately? Is a subtype known? Was the retina dilated or photographed? What change would warrant follow-up? Which classroom cues help on the actual materials?

Camouflage is a task-dependent hypothesis

A small human study found an advantage with certain color-camouflaged stimuli. A simulated-viewing study found an overall trichromat advantage for concealed birds and eggs. Target, background, lighting, and real versus simulated observers matter. Neither result establishes a general advantage for Kai. Human study ↗ Simulation study ↗

Can technology change what someone sees?

Filters and assistive tools

Some specialized glasses or contacts alter contrast between particular colors. They do not replace a missing cone class or create typical color vision, and effects vary by person and task. Phone color identifiers can name a sampled color, while labels and patterns improve access for everyone. NEI overview ↗

Research is not a promise

Gene therapy is being investigated for certain severe inherited conditions; that is different from a standard treatment for common red–green deficiency. If a color change is new, worsening, or differs between eyes, clinical evaluation matters because acquired causes can require attention. NEI treatment and research ↗ NEI causes ↗

Glossary

Opsin
A light-sensitive protein in a photoreceptor.
Isochromatic plate
A colored-dot figure designed to probe color discrimination.
Stereopsis
Depth perceived from the difference between images seen by the two eyes.
Fundus
The back of the eye, including retina and optic disc.
Carrier
Someone with a relevant variant on one X in a conditional X-linked model; vision may vary.
